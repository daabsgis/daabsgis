Analyzing the trend of urban expansion in Adentan Municipality from 2000 to 2026

1. Introduction and objectives

1.1 Background context

The Adentan Municipality lies in the northeastern part of the Greater Accra Region and has grown into one of the most rapidly developing residential and commercial suburbs of Accra. This rapid growth, driven by population pressure, rural-urban migration, and real estate expansion, has caused substantial changes to the area's landscape and land use.

1.2 Problem statement

Uncontrolled urban sprawl without strict zoning enforcement results in the rapid conversion of vital ecosystems into impervious surfaces. Key transportation corridors act as catalyst pathways that accelerate this ecological fragmentation. Monitoring these dynamics is crucial for sustainable municipal planning, flood mitigation, and environmental conservation.

1.3 Objective

The main aim of this study is to use multi-temporal remote sensing data to classify, map, and quantify land use and land cover (LULC) changes in the Adentan Municipality over a 26-year period (2000–2026), while also examining how transportation networks have influenced the municipality's growth and development.

2. Methodology and data sources

2.1 Satellite imagery matrix

To accurately assess land use and land cover changes, satellite imagery was obtained from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) covering a 26-year period. Images from four different years, namely 2000, 2010, 2020, and 2026, were selected to capture the temporal changes in the municipality. The datasets for 2000 and 2010 were acquired from the Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM) sensor, while those for 2020 and 2026 were obtained from the Landsat Operational Land Imager (OLI). All the datasets were processed using a Geographic Coordinate System.

2.2 Classification framework

The landscape was categorized into four distinct mutually exclusive land cover classes:

  1. Built-Ups: Residential structures, commercial blocks, paved roads, and asphalt or concrete infrastructure.
  2. Bare Lands: Cleared land parcels, exposed top soils, construction sites, and unpaved pathways.
  3. Vegetation: Dense canopy, shrublands, grasslands, and agricultural buffers.
  4. Water Bodies: Open surface water reservoirs, streams, rivers, and wetland catchments.

3. Spatial expansion trend: transportation corridors as urban catalysts

The classified map layouts reveal a classic structural pattern of ribbon development, where urban growth anchors itself directly to primary transportation arteries before filling inward.

3.1 The Madina-Dodowa Road Axis (Southwest-to-North Origin)

In 2000, most of the urban development in Adentan Municipality was concentrated in the southwestern part of the area. This was mainly because of its proximity to the Madina-Dodowa Road, a major route connecting Accra to the northeastern parts of the region. The easy access provided by this road encouraged the growth of shops, businesses, and dense residential areas along the corridor. Over time, the built-up area gradually expanded northwards towards the Kpone-Katamanso Municipal District as demand for land grew and land prices continued to rise.

3.2 The Accra-Tema Motorway Corridor (Southern/Eastern Expansion)

The Accra-Tema Motorway did pretty much the same thing to the southern and eastern edges of the area. Because it's a fast highway, it made commuting super easy, which instantly turned nearby open fields into prime land for developers. Before long, warehouses, commercial yards, and gated communities started popping up everywhere along the road. This whole setup basically pulled growth away from the old center of town, quickly filling up the southern border with concrete.

4.1 Detailed class analysis

A. Built-Ups

Built up areas covered 44.7% of the municipality in 2000 but increased sharply to 64.81% by 2010 due to the rapid development that occurred along the Madina Dodowa Highway and the Accra Tema Motorway corridors. With improvements in infrastructure, development gradually spread into the remaining open spaces, causing built up land to rise further to 70.06% in 2020 and eventually reach a near saturated level of 71.21% by 2026.

B. Vegetation

The municipality's vegetation cover experienced a dramatic decline over the study period. In 2000, about 30.0% of the municipality was covered by vegetation, most of which was found in the northern parts of the area, away from the major road corridors. However, as urban development expanded northwards along these transport networks, vegetation cover reduced significantly to 14.86% in 2010 and 10.0% in 2020, before declining further to a critically low level of just 2.03% by 2026.

C. Bare Lands

Bare land remained relatively stable at about 19.6% between 2010 and 2020 as undeveloped plots near the major highways were gradually built upon and consolidated. However, by 2026, bare land increased significantly to 26.45%. This sharp rise suggests extensive land clearing and the subdivision of plots in the northern rural fringes of the municipality, largely driven by improved accessibility and the continued expansion of development into previously undeveloped areas.

D. Water Bodies

Surface water features faced severe encroachment. Water coverage plunged from 1.4% in 2000 to 0.31% by 2026. Natural drainage networks adjacent to the transport routes were heavily altered, filled in, or paved over to clear space for commercial and residential construction.

5. Environmental and spatial implications

The empirical data shows three severe structural risks currently facing Adentan Municipality:

  1. Elevated Flash Flood Risk: With about 71.21% of the municipality now covered by concrete and other impervious surfaces, and surface water bodies reduced to just 0.31%, natural infiltration of rainfall into the soil has been severely limited. As a result, large volumes of surface runoff are generated, which often exceed the capacity of drainage systems along the highways, leading to frequent flash flooding within the municipality.
  2. Rising Air Temperature Trends: The near-total elimination of canopy vegetation from 30.0% down to 2.03% strips the municipality of its natural microclimate cooling and shade. The asphalt matrix absorbs solar radiation, elevating local surface temperatures.
  3. Destabilized slopes: The increase in bare land to 26.45% in 2026 suggests extensive land clearing along the northern hillside areas. This has exposed the soil, making it more vulnerable to erosion and increasing the risk of mudslides during heavy rainfall seasons.

6. Conclusion and recommendations

The multi-temporal LULC data from 2000 to 2026 shows that the development of Adentan Municipality was largely shaped by its transport network. The Madina Dodowa Road and the Tema Motorway acted as key drivers of growth, directing urban expansion across the area. Over time, this changed the municipality from a mixed urban-rural landscape with ecological balance into a highly dense, built-up metropolitan dominated by asphalt and infrastructure.

To manage the remaining landscape, three targeted policies must be implemented immediately:

Establish a Greenbelt Protection Zone: Implement strict regulations to limit the issuance of building permits in ecologically sensitive areas across the municipality to protect the remaining 2.03% vegetation cover and also reduce further land degradation and uncontrolled urban expansion.

Enforce Mandatory Permeable Compound Zoning: Update local building codes to ensure that all new residential and commercial developments maintain at least 30% of each plot as permeable or vegetated ground. This will support natural drainage, reduce surface runoff, and improve stormwater management across the municipality.

Establish Highway Riparian Buffer Zones: Designate strict no-build buffer areas along secondary roads where they intersect with natural water channels and drainage corridors. This will prevent construction activities such as land filling within these sensitive valleys and help protect the natural drainage system from further disturbance.
